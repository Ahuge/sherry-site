Message from Dr Innes

A personal account of why emergency care delays demand system change.

Why do we need No Patient Left Behind? During 40 years as an emergency physician, I dealt with many waiting room disasters and met many families who lost their loved ones because of emergency department (ED) care delays. These people are the inspiration for No Patient Left Behind. This story began 25 years ago when I was working in a Vancouver emergency waiting room. Every chair was full. People were moaning. Someone was on the floor vomiting in a wastebasket. Two cops were restraining a psychotic patient in the corner. People were milling about in obvious pain and someone was yelling obscenities. This was the daily norm, the staff said, even before the pandemic. It just got much worse after. It was obvious that patients were being failed by a system that was incapable of caring for them. When I asked the hospital's ED chief how such bad care could be tolerated in this day and age, he just smiled and said: "Our ED was designed to be able to handle 25,000 patient visits per year, but we are now receiving 55,000. We simply cannot deliver care, and we are doing the best we can." When I asked the hospital CEO why such a situation was allowed to persist year after year, he replied: "Our hospital can't fix the emergency department alone. We can't just add more doctors, nurses, and beds; that would be far too expensive. To improve, we need the entire healthcare system to perform better."

I believed that something was wrong with both the ED chief's and the CEO's response. I believed that hospitals could be managed to improve patient care. So, I dedicated the next 20 years of my career to emergency patient access to improve patient flow through the emergency department. And I discovered that small hospital changes can make a big difference to emergency patient access, including critical care access. I studied the care of 52 Ontario hospitals, and with my students, I published many papers documenting that emergency patient care delays, and subsequent bad outcomes are clearly linked to hospital bed capacity shortages.

In Ontario, we demonstrated that patients who experienced long ED waits had increased mortality, longer hospital stays and higher costs. We also found that delayed care for ED patients suffering acute brain or heart emergencies led to death, while for patients with less time-sensitive illness, waits led to substantial increases in length of stay and health care costs.

Why are such long waiting room delays not being fixed in Canadian hospitals? In my view, there are 5 reasons:

Emergency access block is a hidden issue, despite large numbers of people affected. Access block to emergency care was first described in the medical literature over 30 years ago. Emergency access block is defined as delay to emergency care exceeding 8 hours for at least 90% of patients. Since that time, there have been many Canadian studies demonstrating that emergency access block is common. In a 2004 survey, over 60% of Canadian emergency physicians reported experiencing access block daily, with delays exceeding 48 hours. By 2009, all 10 provinces had long waits with many patients in emergency stretchers waiting over 24 hours. During the last 10 years in Ontario alone, hundreds of thousands of acutely ill patients (including an unknown number at high risk) were left in waiting rooms for an average of 46,000 hours per year per hospital. This provides a large opportunity for waiting room disasters, and indeed many occurred. However, the same study determined that the 46,000 hour emergency care gap represented only 1.14% of the corresponding hospital's bed capacity hours, meaning that very small hospital efficiency improvements in the range of 2 to 4% could eliminate the need to send sick arriving patients to waiting rooms. Experts in patient care and hospital management believe that improvements many times this large are possible without significantly stressing hospitals.

Our mission is to support the introduction of health system innovations that improve patient access to appropriate care (right care, right place) in emergency departments, hospitals or communities. No Patient Left Behind believes that by inspiring and training health leaders, by teaching and promoting better care processes, by rewarding high performers, and by lobbying for the introduction of accountability frameworks (care expectations) that will force changes in how things are done, that we can make the health system better and reduce care delays for Canadians.
